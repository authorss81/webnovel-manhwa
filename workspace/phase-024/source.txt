# Chapter 24: The Truth of the Descent

The sky over the High-Tech Park had turned the color of a bruised plum. The yellow, greasy drizzle had slowed, but the swily fog remained dense, clinging to the shattered glass of the office towers like wet cotton. It was a damp, rotting state of the air that made the lungs feel heavy and the skin itch with a faint, chemical burn.

Wang Teng walked along the western boundary of the clean-air zone. His boots, heavy with the reinforced density of his modified skeletal structure, sank slightly into the wet asphalt with each step. Beside him, his shadow stretched out in jagged, uneven lines, its edges twitching as if testing the limit of the Totem of the Iron Hearth’s repelling field. The matte-black plates of his Dragonbone Armor felt natural against his frame, absorbing the cold draft without transferring the chill to his waxy, grey skin.

Just twenty meters beyond the pale green barrier, the wreckage of a twin-engine military jet lay crumpled across the remains of an elevated pedestrian walkway. The metal of its fuselage was torn like wet cardboard, the blue-grey paint scorched and blistered by an intense, flash-frying heat that had not come from a conventional engine fire. The wings were sheared off, their jagged aluminum bones sticking out of the concrete rubble like broken ribs.

Wang Teng stopped at the edge of the barrier. He looked at the wreckage, his mind instantly calculating the mass and structural stress of the collapsed concrete walkway that held the jet's tail section in the air.

"Landlord," Lin Xia's voice came through the low-frequency radio receiver at his collar. She was back in the B1 lobby, her voice thin but functional, stripped of the raw panic that had defined her first week. "The sensors on the lateral wall are registering a heat spike near the overpass. The tenants are nervous. Some of the Vanguard survivors think the Steel-Blood Syndicate is launching a mortar strike."

"The Syndicate does not have mortar capability in this sector," Wang Teng said, his voice flat, dry, and devoid of emotion. "The sound they heard was a structural failure of the walkway. A scout aircraft from the old world has crashed just outside our perimeter. I am retrieving the logistics."

"Understood," she whispered. "I will update the ledger."

He didn't wait for her to ask anything else. He stepped through the pale green light of the barrier, entering the swily fog.

The moment the clear air ended, the chemical stench of sulfur and burnt kerosene hit his face. The moisture in the air began to itch against his waxy skin, but the Cuirass of the Iron Eater absorbed the thermal residue, converting the mild acid rain into a faint, harmless warmth against his chest.

Wang Teng scrambled up the concrete rubble of the collapsed walkway, his three-hundred-pound frame balanced with the precise center of gravity he had calculated before making his first step.

The cockpit of the jet was sheared open, its canopy shattered into thousands of tiny, diamond-like pieces that littered the melted instrument panels. Inside, the pilot was still strapped to the ejection seat, but there was no flesh left. The pilot's body had been transformed into a cluster of brittle, salt-like white crystals that gleamed with a faint, dying mana signature. The system had converted his physical form into raw materials before he could even pull the secondary handle.

Wang Teng reached past the crystallized ribs, his fingers brushing against the heavy, rectangular steel casing of the flight recorder beneath the center console. The casing was hot, painted in a faded international orange, and bore the stamped serial numbers of the old world's defense logistics division.

He grabbed the handle. The metal groaned as he ripped the mounting brackets free from the melted aluminum floorboards, his high physical density making the task as simple as pulling a dry branch from a tree.

[SYSTEM: You have acquired 'Shattered Military Flight Recorder' (Rank F - Damaged)]

Wang Teng turned the orange box in his hands. The casing was heavily dented, the thermal insulation cracked, and the optical storage drive inside was likely fused by the high-density mana radiation that had downed the aircraft. To any other survivor, this was nothing but twelve kilograms of low-grade scrap metal, useless without a military-grade decryption terminal and a functional power grid.

To Wang Teng, it was an unbalanced ledger.

He tucked the heavy box under his arm and made his way back down the collapsed walkway, his boots grinding the salt-like crystals of the pilot's remains into fine white dust.

Wang Teng returned to the B1 security office, his heavy steps vibrating through the concrete floorboards of the lateral corridor. He set the orange flight recorder on the metal workbench. The smell of cold iron, stagnant battery acid, and wet charcoal filled the small, windowless room.

Number Three was standing by the door, his hand resting on the heavy ring of master keys at his belt. He wore his heavy grey canvas coat, his large, scarred frame casting a wide shadow across the concrete wall. "Is it a weapon, Landlord?" he asked, his voice a low, gravelly vibration.

"No," Wang Teng said, pulling five high-density mana cores from his leather pouch. The small, semi-translucent spheres clinked against the metal table, casting a faint, watery blue light on the orange box. "It is a record of an unallocated deficit. Leave us."

Number Three nodded once, his obedience absolute under the terms of his contract, and backed out of the room, closing the heavy steel door with a dull, metallic click.

Wang Teng sat in his steel-framed desk chair. He looked at the damaged flight recorder. His SSS-rank talent, Inequivalent Exchange, did not care about the physical state of the input. It cared about the conceptual potential of the material—the raw information and physical history locked within the molecules of the scrap.

He placed his waxy, grey palm on the dented orange steel. He felt the cold vacuum in his chest open, a silent, ravenous void that demanded structure.

"System," he murmured.

[SYSTEM: SSS-Rank Talent: Inequivalent Exchange activated.]

[Input: 1x Shattered Military Flight Recorder (Rank F) + 5x High-Density Mana Cores (Rank E) + 300 Mana]

[Concept: Decryption & Reconstruction -> Systemic Origins]

[Exchange successful. You have bypassed equivalent limitations.]

[Obtained Item: Recorded History of the 2999th Descent (Rank S - Unique)]

[Attributes: Immutable Archive, Void-Sync Memory, Absolute Historical Veracity]

The orange flight recorder and the five mana cores dissolved instantly, turning into a thick, black oil that pooled on the metal workbench. The oil did not spread; it rose upward, hardening into a thick, heavy book with a cover made of cold, matte-black glass. The surface was cold to the touch, and it carried no title, only a single, vertical line of glowing violet runes that matched the spatial signature of the Abyssal Kiosk.

Wang Teng picked up the ledger. The weight was immense, far heavier than any book of its size should be. When he opened the first page, the glass did not turn like paper; instead, a massive, high-density stream of optical and spatial data projected directly into his optic nerves.

The projection was a rapid, cold succession of historical fragments, spatial coordinates, and audio logs recorded by the defense networks of civilizations that had existed before the first stone of Shenzhen had been laid.

Wang Teng's eyes remained fixed, his pupils contracting into tiny black points as his analytical mind began to process the sheer volume of the data at a speed that made his temples throb with a sharp, burning pain. He did not feel fear; he felt the cold, hard logic of a structural engineer analyzing a systemic failure.

The data started with the First Descent.

It had happened three hundred thousand years ago on a world whose name had been erased from the system's database. The sky had fractured, gravity had failed, and the survivors had been forced into a gamified system of classes, talents, and monsters. They had fought, they had built sanctuaries, and they had struggled to "ascend" to godhood, believing that if they reached the peak of the system, they would save their world from the creeping void.

They had failed. The moment the strongest survivor reached Level 100 and touched the divine throne, the system had initiated a "Season Liquidation." The entire world was scrubbed, its resources harvested, its populations reduced to raw mana essence, and the planet reset to its natural state to allow a new cycle of livestock to grow.

Then came the Second Descent.

Each time, the system played the exact same scenario. It was a perfectly managed cycle of resource cultivation. The human race, and thousands of other sentient races across the cosmos, were nothing but high-density organic batteries. They were allowed to develop technology, culture, and complexity because a complex society produced a much higher quality of mana when it was compressed and liquidated.

But the most chilling part of the record was not the slaughter. It was the commentary.

Embedded within the system logs of the 2999th Descent—which had ended only six months before Earth's integration began—were metadata packets containing rating charts, audience engagement statistics, and dry, bureaucratic notes from entity designations known as "The Producers."

"The battle at the Sector 4 Citadel showed a twenty-two percent increase in viewer engagement after we introduced the Level 15 Acid Wyrm."

"The human survivor 'Sovereign Chen' has failed to reach the required drama threshold. Initiate the planetary purge two weeks ahead of schedule to prepare for the 3000th iteration."

"We need more high-tier talent activations in the next cycle to offset the declining subscription numbers in the outer sectors."

The "Mystery" was not an integration. It was not a test of human potential, nor was it a divine trial.

It was a reality television show. A massive, multidimensional entertainment spectacle designed for the amusement of immortal entities who watched from the safety of the void, betting on which survivor would die first, laughing at the desperate, sweating humans who built sanctuaries out of scrap metal and praised the system for giving them talents.

Wang Teng sat perfectly still, the black glass ledger resting in his lap.

The cold, structural calm that had guided him through his entire career—the logical pragmatism that allowed him to look at the apocalypse as a series of debt sheets and property lines—cracked.

A physical sensation of heat rose in his chest. It was a quiet, concentrated fury that made his fingers twitch against the edge of the metal desk. His jaw tightened, the waxy skin over his cheekbones turning a sharp, bloodless white.

He had built a sanctuary. He had calculated the rent, managed the tenants, and established a self-sustaining local system, believing that if he maintained his margins, he could survive the integration.

But there was no surviving a system designed for liquidation. No matter how many Jersey barriers he built, no matter how many tons of diesel he hoarded, the moment the "season" ended, the producers would press the reset button to clear the stage for the 3001st run.

"They are watching," Wang Teng whispered, his voice a low, dry rasp that sounded like dry leaves scraping against concrete. "They are calculating the drama."

He looked up at the ceiling of the security office, his gaze passing through the concrete slabs, the lobby, the clear-air barrier of the totem, and the yellow sky beyond.

If the system was a show, then the "Gods" were not divine masters. They were merely executives. And like any executive team, they were bound by the constraints of their own production budget.

"You want drama," Wang Teng murmured, his waxy lips curling into a cold, flat line that carried no humor. "I will give you an eviction notice."

He stood up, the black glass book dissolving back into his inventory with a soft, oily hiss. His mind was no longer calculating the rent of the fifty-two survivors in the lobby. He was calculating the cost of an assassination.

To kill a god, one could not use the system's tools. The swords, the spears, the standard spells—they were all licensed assets, code written by the producers themselves. To use a system-granted weapon against a god was like trying to evict a landlord with a lease they had drafted.

He needed "Divine" tier materials that bypassed the system's architecture. Items that were glitched, corrupt, or harvested from the epiphenomenal rifts where the system's code failed to integrate with Earth's physical laws.

He walked out of the security office, his shadow trailing behind him like a dark, living stain.

In the lobby, Lin Xia looked up from her ledger as his heavy footsteps approached. Her eyes widened slightly when she saw his face. The cold, detached indifference was still there, but beneath it lay a quiet, absolute focus that made her skin go cold.

"Landlord?" she asked, her voice trembling. "Is... is there a problem with the western wall?"

"The sanctuary is operating under new parameters," Wang Teng said, stopping by her desk. "You are in charge of the daily ledger. Number Three will handle the physical enforcement. If any tenant fails to maintain their balance, evict them immediately. We no longer have the margin to carry dead weight."

"Where... where are you going?"

"To the Red Zone," Wang Teng said.

Lin Xia’s pen slipped from her fingers, clattering against the wooden desk. "The Red Zone? But... the scouts from the Syndicate said the Futian core is a dead area. The mana density there is over eight hundred units. Anyone who goes in... their flesh turns to glass within minutes."

"The calculations are simple," Wang Teng said, his voice flat and clinical. "The high-density mana in the Futian rift is unstable. It is a structural fracture in the system's integration matrix. That is where the raw materials are."

He turned away from the desk and walked toward the main entrance.

Before he reached the doors, he accessed his status panel.

[SYSTEM: SSS-Rank Talent: Inequivalent Exchange active.]

[Your SSS-rank talent has detected a high-density conceptual anomaly in the Futian District (Red Zone).]

[Anomalous Signal: Fragment of the Shattered Primeval Core (Rank SS)]

[Warning: The environmental radiation in this sector exceeds safe biological limits by 850%.]

Wang Teng ignored the warning. He pulled his Molecular Blade from its scabbard, his fingers tracing the cold, grey edge of the blade. The weapon was sharp, but it was still a system-defined tool. He would need to exchange it for something far worse once he reached the core.

He pushed open the heavy glass doors of the lobby, stepping out into the clear, cool air of the courtyard.

The survivors huddled under the pavilion looked up as he passed. They saw the Landlord, his waxy grey face set like stone, his heavy black armor clinking with every step. They didn't speak. They had learned that when the Landlord moved with this level of purpose, it was best to stay out of his path.

Wang Teng reached the bronze Totem of the Iron Hearth in the center of the courtyard. He paused, looking at the pale green ring of light that held back the yellow, swily fog.

This sanctuary was a stable business. It was a good foundation. But a foundation was useless if the entire planet was scheduled for demolition.

He stepped beyond the pale green barrier, entering the dark, corrosive mist of the city.

The further west he walked, the more the air changed. The yellow grease of the swily fog began to thin, replaced by a deep, copper-red haze that smelled of old copper and ozone. The concrete of the road was no longer cracked; it was melted, turned into smooth, glassy channels that reflected the red sky like frozen pools of blood.

At the three-kilometer mark, his skin began to itch, a faint, systematic alert flashing at the edge of his vision.

[SYSTEM: Severe Mana Contamination detected.]

[Your physical density has absorbed 12 points of radiation. Health-depletion will begin in 120 seconds.]

Wang Teng did not stop. He adjusted the straps of the Cuirass of the Iron Eater, his eyes fixed on the towering, jagged silhouette of the Futian Financial Center in the distance. The building was torn in half, its upper twenty stories floating in the air, held aloft by a swirling vortex of violet and crimson spatial rifts.

The game was on. The producers were watching.

And Wang Teng was going to break their set.
